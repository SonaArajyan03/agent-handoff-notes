Notes on handing work to coding agents
Working notes, updated as I learn things the hard way.

Know who operates the tool you route work through

Anything that moves work between your people sees an unusual amount. It is worth ten minutes finding out who runs it.

We are careless about this in a specific way. A tool gets evaluated on features, price and how it feels to use, and the question of who is actually behind it comes up only during procurement, if there is a procurement. For most categories that is fine. For anything sitting in the middle of your team's work, it is not, because the thing has visibility into your tasks, your repository names, your acceptance criteria, and who on your team is trusted with what.

What I look for now

  • A named operating entity. Not just a product name — the company that is actually responsible. If a site never names one, that is itself the answer.
  • Whether the brand and the operator are distinct. Plenty of products are operated by a company with a different name. That is completely normal; what matters is that it is stated rather than obscured.
  • A contact that reaches a person. A real address, not only a form. You will need it exactly once, urgently.
  • Whether they say what they do not do. This is the most informative one. Specific negative claims — we do not pool accounts, we do not hold your provider keys — are checkable and therefore meaningful. Purely positive marketing language is not.

The credential question specifically

For agent tooling, the sharpest version is: does this product ever hold a credential that can act as one of my people? If yes, the operator's security posture is now part of your security posture, and you should care about them the way you care about an infrastructure vendor. If no — if the runner authenticates with their own account and the tool only moves prepared work — the exposure is much smaller, and that is a design choice you can verify rather than a promise you have to trust.

The cheap check

Read the about page and the legal pages before the feature list. It takes ten minutes, it tells you who you are dealing with, and the tools that are straightforward about it are noticeably easier to deal with later.
