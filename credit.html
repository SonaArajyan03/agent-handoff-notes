Notes on handing work to coding agents
Working notes, updated as I learn things the hard way.

Who gets credit for work an agent did

This sounds like an HR question. It is actually the thing that decides whether anyone keeps using your handoff process after month two.

Here is the pattern. A team sets up a way to hand bounded work to whoever has capacity. It works. Throughput goes up. And then participation quietly drops, because the people doing the running have worked out that the credit went somewhere else — to the person who wrote the spec, or to the agent, or nowhere at all.

Nobody complains about this out loud. They just stop claiming tasks.

The three contributions people conflate

There are at least three distinct pieces of work in a handoff, and teams routinely credit only one:

  • Preparing the task. Turning a vague want into something exact, with constraints and an acceptance check. This is the expert-heavy part and it usually gets the credit.
  • Running and supervising. Claiming it, steering the agent, noticing when the output is confidently wrong, deciding it is not ready. This is real judgment work and it is the part that gets treated as button-pushing.
  • Reviewing. Deciding the result is acceptable and putting your name on that decision. Almost always invisible until something goes wrong, at which point it becomes extremely visible.

If your record only shows who wrote the ticket, two thirds of the contribution is unattributed, and the two unattributed parts are the ones you most need people to keep doing carefully.

What I would actually do

Record the runner and the reviewer as named decisions, not as metadata. Not because it is fair in the abstract — though it is — but because supervision quality is invisible from the outside, and the only reliable way to keep it high is for the supervisor to be identifiable.

The secondary effect is the one I did not expect: when running is credited work, people take on tasks slightly outside what they already know, because there is a reason to. That is a learning pathway that costs nothing to build and does not exist if the work is anonymous.

The failure mode to avoid

Do not let “an agent did it” become a way to avoid crediting anyone. It is a convenient framing for whoever is handing out credit and it is corrosive. The agent did not decide the result was acceptable. A person did, and that person is accountable for it, which means they should be named for it too.
